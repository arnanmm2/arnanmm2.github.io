Iran and Oman near deal to restore commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz

Iran and Oman are reportedly close to finalizing a framework agreement, reported 6-7 August, to restore commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz after months of disruption since the 2026 Iran war began in February. The framework would require vessels to pay maritime service fees through a newly created corporation rather than a full, unconditional reopening. President Trump said the broader Iran war could end "pretty soon," even as Abu Dhabi National Oil Co. reported continued attacks on transiting vessels. Roughly 13 million barrels a day — about a fifth of global oil consumption — normally moves through the strait.

InsightIndonesia imports a large share of its crude oil and LNG through Gulf routes feeding Hormuz, so any new fee regime or continued disruption keeps landed fuel costs elevated for Pertamina and the state subsidy budget — a durable reopening would ease one of the biggest tail risks currently priced into global energy markets.

DRC Ebola outbreak becomes the country's largest ever recorded, over 3,600 confirmed cases

The Ebola Bundibugyo virus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has reached 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths as of WHO's 1 August update — a 44% case-fatality ratio — surpassing the 2018-2020 outbreak's 3,317 cases to become the country's largest ever. The outbreak spans 49 health zones across five provinces, with active transmission in 33. No vaccine or specific treatment exists for this Ebola species, though candidates are being tested; Uganda separately declared its own linked outbreak over on 28 July. InsightIndonesia has no direct exposure, but as a participant in international health regulations and a country prone to its own zoonotic-disease risks, it has a real stake in how WHO's emergency response and vaccine-access frameworks perform in a crisis of this scale.

Why the IDX Composite (IHSG) is still down roughly 26% year-to-date — even after a strong summer rebound

TL;DR — The IHSG opened 2026 near an all-time high of 9.134,70 (20 Jan), then fell as low as 5.594,76 by early June — a five-year low — before rebounding to 6.409,65 by 7 August. The index remains one of the weakest major-market performers of 2026 even as its recent trajectory has clearly turned positive.
The mechanism

Much of 2026's earlier IHSG selloff traced back to a hawkish global rate backdrop and foreign-portfolio outflows from emerging markets generally. That pressure has eased since: US July payrolls badly missed expectations, cutting the odds the Fed hikes again, while Bank Indonesia's decision to hold its own policy rate at 5,75% (rather than hike further) has kept the rupiah relatively stable near Rp17.919/USD. Foreign inflows into Indonesian government securities (SBN) and BI's SRBI instruments have picked back up as a result, helping fuel the IHSG's five straight weeks of gains into August.

Why it matters to you

The infrastructure sector led Friday's rally (+2.17%), alongside strong Q2 investment-realization data (Rp511.8 trillion, +7.1% y/y) and the startup of the Rp80.6 trillion Pomalaa nickel-battery smelter — signs that domestic fundamentals are reasserting themselves after a rough first half. Still, the index sits roughly 26% below its January high, a reminder that a summer rebound is not the same as a full recovery.

Bitcoin near $65K: still a macro asset tracking equities, not an independent hedge — and the Indonesia angle

TL;DR — Bitcoin rallied roughly 10% in July, approaching the $65,000 resistance zone, and is trading around $64,900-65,000 in early August — up about 3% over the past week. August is historically Bitcoin's weakest month, so analysts are split on whether the rally extends or cools.
The mechanism

The same forces moving emerging-market currencies and equities — Fed rate expectations, the dollar's strength, and broader risk appetite — are also driving Bitcoin. A Fed that held rather than hiked in late July, plus a weak US jobs report reducing near-term hike odds, has kept risk assets including crypto broadly supported, even as seasonal and technical patterns specific to August create some headwind.

Why it matters to you

Two things follow for an Indonesian investor. (1) Holding both IDX stocks and BTC still offers less diversification than it appears — both assets are currently being lifted by the same softer-dollar, risk-on backdrop. (2) A rupiah still down 7.5% YTD can keep pulling some local demand toward BTC/USD as a multi-year store-of-value thesis, even as short-term price action stays correlated with US equities. Crypto in Indonesia is regulated as a financial asset under OJK and trades are taxed — factor that in before sizing any position.

Deep dives — the mechanisms behind today's headlines

Why a weak jobs report just sent the stock market to record highs

TL;DR — Fewer new jobs usually signals a cooling economy. But markets rallied because weak job growth makes it less likely the Fed will hike interest rates further — and richly-valued tech/AI stocks are priced on the assumption that borrowing costs will stay contained.
The mechanism

Growth-stock valuations lean heavily on future profits discounted back to today's dollars. The interest rate used for that discounting is anchored to the Fed's policy rate — so when the Fed is expected to hold or eventually cut rather than hike, those future profits are worth more in today's terms, and the stocks priced most aggressively on future growth (semiconductors, AI infrastructure, software) benefit most. That's exactly what happened on 7 August: July payrolls fell 23,000 versus an expected +80,000, all but erasing near-term hike odds, and the Nasdaq jumped 1.3% to a fresh high while the S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64.

Why it matters to you

This "Fed on hold" dynamic is part of why the rupiah steadied near Rp 17,919 and the IHSG rallied 1.04% on the very same trading day (see the chain above, and the Currency tab). One headline — a U.S. jobs number — touches your stock portfolio, your import prices, and your borrowing costs all at once.

Go deeper: U.S. jobs data — bls.gov/ces (Employment Situation report) · Fed policy expectations — federalreserve.gov (FOMC calendar & statements) · how discount rates affect stock valuations — any introductory finance text on "discounted cash flow" (DCF) valuation.

The Strait of Hormuz: how one narrow channel can move oil prices everywhere

TL;DR — The Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and Oman, is the narrow sea passage that roughly a fifth of the world's oil (and a large share of LNG) must sail through. Even talk of reopening it after months of disruption moves prices — including for net energy importers like Indonesia.
The mechanism

Geography makes Hormuz a "chokepoint": there's no practical alternative sea route for Gulf oil exporters, so even partial disruption forces tankers to either wait, pay sharply higher war-risk insurance, or take long, costly detours. Markets price this risk in immediately — and they also price in relief when disruption eases. In early August, Iran and Oman moved close to a framework restoring commercial shipping through the strait, even as Abu Dhabi's national oil company reported continued attacks on transiting vessels — a mixed signal that kept Brent oscillating near $83-84/bbl rather than settling clearly in either direction.

Why it matters to you

Indonesia imports a substantial share of its crude oil and LNG. When Hormuz-driven risk pushes oil prices up, that cost flows almost directly into Pertamina's pump prices and into the broader inflation figures that shape Bank Indonesia's interest-rate decisions — currently a policy rate held at 5.75% since the 21-22 July meeting.

Go deeper: U.S. Energy Information Administration's "World Oil Transit Chokepoints" briefs (eia.gov) explain Hormuz and other chokepoints (Strait of Malacca, Suez Canal — both also critical for Indonesia's own trade routes) with maps and volume data.

From the Fed to your wallet: how U.S. interest rates move the Rupiah

TL;DR — When U.S. interest rates are expected to stay high (or rise further) relative to other countries, global investors can earn more by holding dollars — so money flows out of emerging markets like Indonesia and into the U.S., weakening currencies such as the rupiah. When that expectation eases, the reverse can happen.
The mechanism

Large investors constantly compare returns across countries. If a U.S. government bond pays meaningfully more than an equivalent Indonesian asset (after adjusting for risk), capital tends to flow toward the higher-paying, lower-risk option — the U.S. This is sometimes called the "yield differential" effect. Under Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, the Fed held its rate at ~3.6% on 28-29 July with three dissents; a weak July jobs report released 7 August then cut the odds of any further hike, easing dollar strength broadly — not just against the rupiah but against the euro and Singapore dollar too, which is roughly the pattern the indexed chart in the Currency tab shows.

Why it matters to you

This is the macro backdrop behind the rupiah steadying near Rp 17,919/USD by 6 August — still down ~7.5% YTD, but well off its mid-year weak point — as Bank Indonesia holds its own policy rate at 5.75%. It's a clear example of BI managing a force that originates largely outside Indonesia's borders: when the Fed backs off a hike, BI's job of defending the rupiah gets a little easier.

Go deeper: Bank Indonesia's policy statements and "Kurs Transaksi BI" data — bi.go.id · for the textbook version of this mechanism, search "interest rate parity" and "carry trade" in any international-finance resource.

Reading an earthquake bulletin like a scientist: from "M7.7" to this week's "M6.4 near Karatung"

TL;DR — Magnitude is a logarithmic scale — each whole-number step represents roughly 32× more energy released — and an earthquake's depth matters as much as its size for deciding how dangerous it is and whether a tsunami is likely.
The mechanism

An M7.7 earthquake isn't "a bit bigger" than an M6.7 — it releases roughly 32 times more energy, which is why agencies treat anything above M7 as a major event requiring an immediate tsunami assessment. Depth is the second critical variable: shallow undersea earthquakes are far more likely to displace large volumes of seawater and generate tsunami waves than very deep ones, because the rupture is closer to the seafloor surface. This week's magnitude-6.4 quake near Pulau Karatung, North Sulawesi (5 August) is a useful case study: at a shallow depth of 10km but sitting in the M6.1-6.9 range that can damage buildings near the epicenter, BMKG still ruled out a tsunami — the combination of magnitude, depth and epicenter location together determined that call, not magnitude alone.

Why it matters to you

Knowing how to read magnitude, depth and tsunami-potential labels means you don't have to wait for a news anchor's tone of voice to judge whether an earthquake alert is serious — you can form your own first impression directly from the bulletin, then turn to BMKG's live feed for the official, continuously-updated tsunami and aftershock status.

Go deeper: BMKG's Indonesia Tsunami Early Warning System — inatews.bmkg.go.id · USGS "Earthquake Hazards" educational pages explain magnitude scales, depth classifications and aftershock sequences with visual examples (earthquake.usgs.gov).
